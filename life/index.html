Heredity and evolution, K to 12

How Life Changes

Every living thing is a recipe that gets copied. Copies have tiny mistakes. Some mistakes help, and the helpful ones get copied more. That is the whole trick, and it made everything alive, from moss to you. Six games let you run the trick yourself.

Living things copy their instructions, and no copy is perfect. Which copies get made again depends on the world they land in. Run that loop long enough and you get eyes, wings, and antibiotic-proof germs. Six games, one for each piece of the loop: copy, select, inherit, multiply, drift, and branch.

Heredity and evolution as six experiments: mutation as copying error, natural selection with you as the predator, Mendelian ratios from real random draws, exponential growth and the rise of resistance, genetic drift in small populations, and phylogenetic trees rebuilt from shared differences. Each is scored by the real probability.

Mutation rates and proofreading, selection coefficients and the change in allele frequency they produce, Hardy–Weinberg and chi-square, logistic growth with mutation, selection and combination therapy, Wright–Fisher drift and effective population size, distance-based tree building with a Jukes–Cantor correction. Every model states its assumptions.

The same six games read as population genetics: the error threshold, the breeder's equation and stabilizing selection, Mendel's ratios as the evidence for particulate inheritance, Luria and Delbrück on the randomness of mutation, coalescent time scales, and the combinatorial size of tree space. Where the textbook picture stops is marked in each chapter.

Chapter 1

Warm-upIf you copied a recipe by hand a hundred times, what would happen to it?

Copy me

Inside every living thing is a recipe written in four letters: A, T, C and G. When a cell splits in two, it copies the whole recipe. Copiers make mistakes. A mistake in the recipe is called a mutation.

Most mistakes do nothing. Some break the recipe. Once in a while one makes it better. Copy the recipe here and count the mistakes. Then turn on proofreading and see what a careful copier can do.

Every copy has mistakes

DNA is a chain of four letters, and a cell copies all of it before it divides: about three billion letters in each of your cells. The copying machine gets roughly one letter in a hundred million wrong, after checking its own work. That sounds small, but three billion letters times a tiny error rate still leaves you with about sixty letters your parents did not have.

Mutations are the raw material. They are random with respect to what would be useful: the copier does not know what the organism needs. Most land in stretches that do not matter, some do harm, and a very few help. The next chapter is about what happens to those.

Mutation is copying error

Mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Point mutations swap one base; insertions and deletions shift the reading frame; larger events copy or lose whole stretches. The per-base rate per generation is about 10⁻⁸ in humans and about 10⁻¹⁰ per base per division in bacteria, but bacteria divide so often, and there are so many of them, that new resistance mutations appear inside a single infected patient.

In the lab, the expected number of differences after g copies of an L-letter sequence at error rate e is e·L·g, as long as the differences are few. When they are many, the same letter can change twice and the count saturates; the lab shows that too.

Mutation rates and proofreading

DNA polymerase alone mis-pairs about 1 in 10⁵ bases. Its 3′→5′ exonuclease proofreading removes about 99% of those, and post-replication mismatch repair another 99%, giving the observed 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹⁰ per base per division. Mutator strains that lose one layer have rates a hundred times higher, and they turn up in chronic infections and in tumours, where a high mutation supply is temporarily an advantage.

Mutation is not uniform along the sequence: transitions outnumber transversions about two to one, CpG sites mutate an order of magnitude faster through deamination of methylcytosine, and replication timing and chromatin state modulate the rate. The lab's uniform error rate is the first approximation.

The error threshold

Eigen's error threshold: a replicator of length L with per-base error e keeps its information only if e·L ≲ ln σ, where σ is the selective advantage of the master sequence. Above that, the population melts into a cloud of mutants and the best sequence is lost. RNA viruses with L ≈ 10⁴ live near e ≈ 10⁻⁴, right at the edge, which is why mutagenic drugs such as favipiravir and molnupiravir can push them over it.

Mutation rate itself evolves. The drift-barrier hypothesis holds that selection lowers it until the marginal benefit is smaller than 1/N_e, which is why large-population microbes have lower per-base rates than large animals, and why per-genome-per-generation rates are surprisingly similar across life.

For grown-ups

Kids may worry that mistakes are bad. Say it plainly: most do nothing, a few are bad, a very few are good, and the good ones are how we got here.

Chapter 2

Warm-upA bird eats the moths it can see. What happens to the moths it cannot see?

The moth game

Moths rest on tree bark all day. Birds eat the ones they can see. A moth that matches the bark is hard to spot, so it lives, and has babies that look like it.

You are the bird. Eat as many moths as you can before the time runs out. Then watch what the next generation looks like. Then make the bark sooty and play again.

The bird chooses

In England in the 1800s, factory smoke blackened the trees, and within fifty years the peppered moths near the cities were almost all dark. When the air was cleaned up, the pale ones came back. Nobody changed the moths. Birds ate the easy-to-see ones, and the survivors bred.

That is natural selection: variation, inheritance, and a difference in who survives. It needs no plan and no goal. The bird is not trying to make the moths dark. It is just hungry.

Natural selection

Natural selection needs three things: variation in a trait, heritability of that trait, and a difference in survival or reproduction linked to it. In the lab the trait is shade, offspring inherit their parent's shade with a little noise, and you (or the automatic bird) remove the ones you can see. The population mean tracks the bark within a handful of generations.

Kettlewell's experiments on Biston betularia in the 1950s, and Majerus's seven-year replication published in 2012, measured exactly this: predation by birds, differing by background, strong enough to explain the historical change.

Selection coefficients

Write the survival of the two types as w_dark and w_pale and the frequency of dark as p. After one round of selection, p′ = p·w_dark ÷ (p·w_dark + q·w_pale). With relative fitnesses 1 and 1 − s, the change per generation is Δp ≈ s·p·q for small s: fastest at intermediate frequency and slow at both ends, which is the S-shaped curve the lab plots.

For a continuous trait like shade, the response to selection is the breeder's equation R = h²·S: the shift in the next generation equals the heritability times the difference between the survivors' mean and the population mean. The lab's inheritance rule sets h² near one; real traits are lower, and the lab reports both S and R so you can read h² off.

Stabilizing selection

The moth story is directional selection. Set the bark to grey and the same machinery becomes stabilizing: extremes are eaten, the mean stays put, and the variance shrinks each generation until mutation replenishes it. Most selection in nature is of this kind, which is why populations look stable while selection is strong.

The genetics behind the moth is known: the dark morph is a single transposable-element insertion in the cortex gene, dated to about 1819, that reached 98% frequency near Manchester by 1895. A selection coefficient near 0.2 fits both the rise and the decline.

For grown-ups

Let them play several rounds before you explain anything. The population changing colour without anyone deciding it is the discovery.

Chapter 3

Warm-upTwo tall pea plants have a short baby. How is that possible?

Pea flips

Every pea plant carries two copies of the 'how tall' instruction, one from each parent. A baby gets one copy from each. Some copies say tall (T) and some say short (t). If a plant has even one tall copy, it grows tall.

Pick the parents and grow the babies. Count tall and short. Grow more and see what number the count keeps landing near.

Two copies of every instruction

Gregor Mendel counted thousands of pea plants in the 1860s and found that traits do not blend: they come in units, now called genes, and each plant carries two copies, called alleles. A tall parent with copies T and t passes on T or t with equal chance, like a coin flip. Cross two Tt parents and you get TT, Tt, tT and tt in equal numbers: three tall for every one short.

The Punnett square in the lab is just the four coin-flip outcomes. The lab draws real random babies, so the count wobbles: grow a hundred and it lands near 3 to 1; grow four and it might not.

Mendel's ratios

Mendel's law of segregation: the two alleles separate into gametes with equal probability. Independent assortment: alleles at different genes segregate independently when they sit on different chromosomes. A dihybrid TtRr × TtRr therefore gives 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Dominance is not the rule but a common case; many alleles are codominant or incompletely dominant, and most traits involve many genes.

Statistics matter here. A 3 : 1 ratio from 4 plants is weak evidence; from 400 it is strong. The lab reports the deviation from expectation as the sample grows.

Hardy–Weinberg

Scale up from a cross to a population. If alleles T and t have frequencies p and q with no selection, mutation, migration or drift, and mating is random, genotype frequencies are p², 2pq and q², and they stay there: the Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. It is the null model of population genetics; departures from it are how selection, inbreeding and population structure are detected.

A recessive trait seen in 4% of a population implies q = 0.2 and carriers 2pq = 32%. That arithmetic is the basis of genetic counselling, and of the observation that rare recessive alleles hide in heterozygotes where selection can barely touch them.

Why the ratios mattered

Mendel's ratios were the first hard evidence that inheritance is particulate rather than blending, which removes the strongest objection to Darwin: that variation would be halved every generation. Fisher showed in 1918 that many Mendelian loci of small effect produce exactly the continuous, normally distributed traits and parent–offspring correlations the biometricians had measured. That reconciliation is the modern synthesis.

Fisher also noted that Mendel's counts fit expectation too well, with an improbably small χ²; the debate continues. The lab's honest random draws show what real sampling noise looks like.

For grown-ups

Two coins per parent make a physical version of this: heads is T, tails is t. Flip, record, repeat.

Chapter 4

Warm-upWhy does a doctor tell you to finish all the pills even when you feel better?

The bacteria race

A germ splits into two. Then those two split. Then four, eight, sixteen. In a few hours one germ becomes millions. Medicine kills germs, but if a single germ happens to be tough enough to survive, it keeps splitting, and soon every germ is the tough kind.

Fill the dish, then clear it with medicine. Then try stopping the medicine early and see what grows back.

Doubling and resistance

Bacteria double about every 20 minutes when they are fed. Ten cells become ten thousand in a little over three hours. Among ten thousand copies, the copying mistakes from chapter 1 mean a few cells are different by chance, and once in a while one is different in a way that shrugs off an antibiotic.

The antibiotic kills the ordinary cells and leaves that one. It now has the whole dish to itself. That is why doctors say to finish the course: stopping early is exactly the experiment that breeds resistance, and it is the same natural selection as the moths, with the drug as the bird.

Exponential growth and selection

Unlimited growth is N = N₀·2^(t/T) with doubling time T; with a resource limit K it bends into the logistic curve. Resistance mutations arise at a rate μ per division, so a culture that has undergone D divisions has had about μ·D chances, whether or not the drug was present. Add the drug and the sensitive cells die exponentially while the resistant ones keep growing: a selective sweep in real time.

Resistance usually costs something, so without the drug the sensitive strain can outgrow it again. The lab gives resistant cells a 10% slower doubling; watch what happens when you stop dosing after resistance has won.

Mutation, growth and combination therapy

Two independent resistance mutations at 10⁻⁶ each arise together at 10⁻¹², so a culture of 10⁹ cells almost surely contains single mutants and almost surely no double mutants. Give two drugs at once and no cell can escape; give them one after the other and each sweep produces the next mutant's population. That is the logic of combination therapy for tuberculosis and HIV. Try to clear a dish that resistance has already taken over.

The model is deterministic in growth and stochastic in mutation: births per step are r·N·dt, and mutant cells appear as Poisson events with mean μ times births. The lab's μ is deliberately high, about 7 × 10⁻⁵ per division, so that a dish of ten thousand breeds a resistant mutant about half the time.

Luria and Delbrück

Luria and Delbrück asked in 1943 whether mutations arise in response to the drug or before it. If induced, every culture gives about the same number of resistant colonies: Poisson, variance about equal to the mean. If spontaneous, a mutation early in growth is inherited by a large clone, and cultures vary wildly, with variance far above the mean. They found the jackpots. Run the fluctuation test here and read the variance.

The same distribution now underlies estimates of mutation rates, the timing of pre-existing resistance in tumours before chemotherapy, and the question of how many independent origins a resistance allele has.

For grown-ups

The 'finish the course' message is real and lives in this game. Let a child stop the medicine early on purpose and see what grows back.

Chapter 5

Warm-upIf ten animals each have a baby, and colour does not matter, can a colour still disappear?

Drift island

On a small island, colour does not matter to anyone. Every baby copies a random parent. That is all. Yet after a while one colour is gone, just by luck. The fewer animals, the faster it happens.

Run a tiny island and a big one. Then press the storm button that leaves only five survivors, and see what luck does to the colours.

Luck in small numbers

Selection is not the only thing that changes a population. Chance does too. If only ten animals breed, the next generation is a small sample, and small samples wobble: flip ten coins and you often get seven heads. Over many generations the wobble adds up until one version wins for no reason at all. That is genetic drift.

Drift is strongest in small populations, after a disaster (a bottleneck), or when a few animals found a new colony. It is why island species and endangered ones lose variety, and why not every difference between species is an adaptation.

Genetic drift

With N gene copies and a colour at frequency p, the next generation's frequency is a random draw with variance p(1 − p)/N. Given enough generations the frequency hits 0 or 1 and stays: fixation. A neutral version's chance of eventually fixing equals its current frequency, so most new neutral mutations are lost within a few generations, and the ones that do fix take on the order of N generations to do it.

Bottlenecks and founder events shrink N for a while, which is enough. The northern elephant seal, hunted to about twenty animals in the 1890s, has almost no genetic variation today although there are two hundred thousand of them.

Wright–Fisher and effective size

The lab is the Wright–Fisher model: non-overlapping generations, constant N, and the next generation sampled with replacement from the parents. Heterozygosity decays by a factor (1 − 1/N) per generation for N gene copies. Real populations have unequal sex ratios, variable family sizes and fluctuating size; the effective population size N_e is the Wright–Fisher N that would give the same decay, and it is usually far below the census count. For humans it is about 10⁴ despite eight billion of us.

Drift and selection compete: a version with selection coefficient s behaves nearly neutrally when |s| is smaller than about 1/N_e. In small populations harmful versions can fix and beneficial ones can be lost, which is the basis of the nearly neutral theory.

Coalescent time

Run the same process backwards and you get the coalescent: any two gene copies in a population of N copies share an ancestor on average N generations ago, and a whole sample coalesces in about 2N. This is the machine behind every 'most recent common ancestor' date, from mitochondrial Eve at roughly 150,000 years to the dating of SARS-CoV-2 lineages, and its assumptions, constant size and no selection, are exactly the ones the lab lets you break.

Bottlenecks compress coalescence times, which is why the expansion out of Africa shows up as a loss of diversity with distance from Africa, and why a recovering population keeps the genetic signature of its worst year for tens of thousands of years.

For grown-ups

Beads in a bag reproduce this exactly: draw ten with replacement to make the next generation.

Chapter 6

Warm-upHow could you tell that a whale is a closer cousin of a hippo than of a shark?

Cousins

Every living thing has a recipe, and cousins have recipes that look alike, because they copied from the same grandparent. The more the recipes match, the closer the cousins. That is how we know a whale is closer to a hippo than to a shark.

Five creatures. Compare their stripes. Find the two closest cousins, then keep joining until you have the whole family tree.

Shared mistakes make a family tree

In the history board you saw that copies sharing the same mistake come from the same earlier copy. DNA works the same way. Two species that share a copying mistake got it from a shared ancestor, and the number of differences between them counts the time since they split. Line up the letters, count the differences, join the closest pair, repeat.

This is how the tree of life is built. It is why we can say that birds are dinosaurs, that fungi are closer to us than to plants, and that every living thing shares one ancestor from about four billion years ago.

Building a phylogeny

A phylogeny is a hypothesis about ancestry. The simplest method is the one in the lab: compute a distance between every pair of sequences, join the closest pair, average their distances to everything else, and repeat. That is UPGMA, and it assumes a clock: changes accumulate at the same rate on every branch. More careful methods (neighbour joining, maximum likelihood, Bayesian inference) drop that assumption and use a model of how letters change.

The signal is shared derived characters. Shared ancestral ones say nothing about who is closer, and convergence, the same feature evolving twice, can mislead. Sequences reduce but do not eliminate this.

Distances and the molecular clock

Observed differences under-count real changes because a site can change twice. The Jukes–Cantor correction, d = −¾·ln(1 − 4p/3), inverts the simplest model of substitution; p saturates at 0.75 and the corrected distance goes to infinity, which is why deep splits are hard to date from fast-evolving sequences. A molecular clock calibrates d against fossils: a rate r per lineage gives a split age of d ÷ 2r.

Rates vary among lineages, genes and sites. Relaxed-clock models let each branch draw its own rate from a distribution, and mixing genes with different rates, slow ribosomal RNA for deep splits and fast mitochondrial DNA for recent ones, is standard practice.

The size of tree space

The number of rooted binary trees on n leaves is (2n − 3)!!: 105 for five species, about 3 × 10⁷⁴ for fifty. No method can examine them all, so tree search is heuristic, and the honest output is a posterior distribution over trees rather than one answer. Gene trees also disagree with each other through incomplete lineage sorting and horizontal transfer, so 'the' tree of life is a statistical summary, and near its root, where transfer dominated, a network.

This is the manuscript stemma of the history board made rigorous: characters shared by descent, a substitution model, and a likelihood over trees.

For grown-ups

Younger kids can stay with 'more matching stripes means closer cousins'. The tree itself is optional at K to 5.

The end, for now

That is the whole recipe for the recipe. It has no plan, no goal and no author, and in four billion years of copying it wrote every living thing, including the one reading this.

Copy, with mistakes. Keep what works. Repeat.
