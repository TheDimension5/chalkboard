Thermoelectric power, K to 12

Hot Side, Cold Side

A fire is warm. A snowbank is cold. Put something between them that likes a difference, and you get electricity, with no moving parts at all. This board is about that trick: where heat goes, what it takes to boil a pot, how to keep a fire just right, and how to squeeze watts out of a hot side and a cold side.

Heat always flows from hot to cold. A thermoelectric module is a slab that turns some of that flow into electricity while it passes through, which is why a wood stove, a camp fire, a pot of water, or even your hand can power something. Seven games: from heat flowing between two blocks to a full system that charges a phone from a stove and warms your water with the leftover.

Conduction, specific and latent heat, combustion power, the Seebeck effect, the Carnot limit and the thermoelectric figure of merit ZT, maximum power transfer, and a system design that budgets watts, temperatures and time. Every game is scored by the real equations, and every number comes with its unit.

Thermal resistance networks from source to sink, energy balances on stoves and pots, the coupled Seebeck–Fourier description of a module, efficiency as Carnot times a ZT-dependent factor, impedance matching to a load and to a boost converter, and combined heat and power as a design problem rather than a gadget.

Thermoelectrics as coupled transport: Onsager symmetry between the Seebeck and Peltier coefficients, ZT as the single dimensionless number that bounds efficiency, why ZT ≈ 1 has been the wall for seventy years, and what the phonon-glass electron-crystal idea does about it. Plus the engineering that decides real output: interface resistance, sink design, and cold-side runaway.

Chapter 1

Warm-upTouch a warm cup. Which way is the heat going, and when does it stop?

Heat flows downhill

Touch a hot cup and your hand warms up. Heat moves from the hot thing to the cold thing, always, until they match. Hot and cold together is what a difference looks like, and a difference never lasts on its own.

Join a hot block and a cold block with a bar and watch them meet in the middle. Then keep one side hot and the other cold and watch the difference stay.

Heat moves from hot to cold

Heat is energy on the move, and it moves one way: from the hotter thing to the colder one. How fast depends on the difference, the material (copper carries heat fast, wood barely at all), and the shape of the path. A thick, short copper bar moves heat quickly; a thin, long wooden one hardly moves it.

Left alone, the two blocks end up at the same temperature and the flow stops. To keep heat flowing you must keep the hot side hot and the cold side cold, which is the whole job of a stove on one side and water or fins on the other.

Conduction

Fourier's law: the rate of heat flow through a bar is Q̇ = kA(T_hot − T_cold)/L, with k the thermal conductivity (copper 400, steel 50, wood 0.15 W/m·K), A the cross-section and L the length. Each block changes temperature at a rate dT/dt = ±Q̇/(mc), where c is its specific heat.

Without a source and a sink the difference decays exponentially. The time constant is the product of the bar's thermal resistance and the blocks' heat capacity, the same form as an RC circuit.

Thermal resistance networks

Everything on this board is a series of thermal resistances, R = L/(kA) in K/W, between a source and a sink. A temperature difference divides across them like voltage across resistors in series, and the heat flow is the current. A thermoelectric module is one resistor in that chain, and it only sees the share of the difference that falls across it.

Contact resistance at every interface is usually the largest term and the least advertised: a dry, rough joint can cost more difference than the module itself.

The heat equation and its limits

Fourier's law is the linear-response, local-equilibrium limit of transport; it fails at nanoscale (ballistic phonons, Kapitza resistance at interfaces) and at very short times (hyperbolic corrections). For a stove and a slab it is exact enough, and the lumped model here is its one-node discretisation.

Entropy production in conduction is Q̇(1/T_c − 1/T_h): the difference is being spent whether or not anything useful is extracted. A thermoelectric module intercepts part of that spending, which is the second-law view of chapter 4.

For grown-ups

The one-sentence version: heat is a difference being spent. Everything on the board is about making that difference and keeping it.

Chapter 2

Warm-upWhy does a big pot take longer to boil than a small one?

Boiling water

To make water hot you have to give it energy, and a lot of it. Warming a litre of water by one degree takes about as much energy as lifting a bag of sugar up to the roof of a house. Boiling it takes a hundred of those. Turning it into steam takes five hundred more.

Put a pot on the fire and watch the numbers. A bigger fire boils it faster. More water takes longer.

Specific heat and boiling

Every kilogram of water needs 4,186 joules to warm by one degree. Warming 1 kg from 20 °C to 100 °C takes 80 × 4,186 ≈ 335,000 J. A 500 W fire delivers 500 J every second, so that is about 670 seconds, eleven minutes, if every joule reaches the water.

Then it boils, and the temperature stops rising. All the fire's energy now goes into turning water into steam, 2,260,000 J per kilogram, which is why a pot boils dry slowly and why steam burns are so bad.

Q = mcΔT, then latent heat

Sensible heating: Q = m c ΔT with c = 4,186 J/(kg·K). Boiling: Q = m L with L = 2.26 MJ/kg. Time at power P is t = Q/P, and a real fire under a real pot delivers only 20 to 40% of its heat to the water; the rest warms the air.

Predict before you boil: 1 L from 20 °C at 800 W of delivered power is 335 kJ ÷ 800 W = 419 s, seven minutes. Then check your error.

Energy balance on a pot

The pot is a control volume: P_in − losses = mc dT/dt below boiling and P_in − losses = L dm/dt at the boil. Losses grow with temperature (convection and radiation from the pot and the free surface), so the last ten degrees take longer than the first ten. A lid cuts evaporative loss, which dominates near the boil.

Efficiency of heat transfer from flame to water is the ratio of the water's energy gain to the fuel's energy release; for an open fire it is often below 10%, for a good stove 30%, for a kettle element over 90%.

Phase change as a heat sink

At the boil the water is an isothermal reservoir: it absorbs energy at 100 °C without changing temperature, which is exactly what a thermoelectric cold side wants. Boiling-water cooling of the cold face is one of the oldest camp-stove generator designs, and a thermosiphon with a phase-change loop needs no pump.

The latent heat of vaporisation falls to zero at the critical point (374 °C, 22 MPa); near it, small heat inputs produce large volume changes, the operating regime of supercritical steam plants.

For grown-ups

Time a real kettle with a stopwatch and a measuring jug; the lab's numbers should be within a minute of it.

Chapter 3

Warm-upWhat is a log made of, and where did its energy come from?

The wood fire

A log is stored sunshine. Burn it and the energy comes out as heat: a lot of heat, more than a thousand light bulbs' worth from one log while it burns. The trick with a stove is not making it hot but keeping it just right: hot enough to be useful, not so hot that things break.

Feed the stove. Keep the top in the green zone. Too many logs and it runs away.

Fuel energy and burn rate

Dry wood holds about 16 million joules per kilogram. Burn a kilogram in an hour and the fire is running at 16,000,000 ÷ 3,600 ≈ 4,400 W of heat. A hot camp fire can burn ten times that. A thermoelectric module on top can only use a tiny part of it, and it must not get above about 170 °C or it is damaged.

So a stove for making electricity is throttled: enough fire to hold the surface in a band, with the rest of the heat going into the room or into water.

A stove as a thermal system

Heat release P_fire = ṁ_fuel × H, with H ≈ 16 MJ/kg for dry wood. The stove's surface temperature settles where heat in equals heat out: losses to the room, to the flue, and into whatever sits on top. The lab models the stove as one thermal mass with a loss rate proportional to (T − T_room).

Hold the top between 150 and 170 °C, the sweet spot for a bismuth-telluride module. Overshoot and the module's solder and semiconductor degrade; undershoot and the output falls with the square of the difference.

Combustion and control

Wood burns in stages: drying, pyrolysis (volatiles), char oxidation. Real heating value depends on moisture: 20% moisture wood delivers about 20% less than dry, since water must be boiled off first. Excess air, flue temperature and draft set how much of the release reaches the stove body rather than the chimney.

Holding a temperature band with a fuel that arrives in lumps is a control problem with delay; the lab's stove has a time constant of minutes, so stoking is a matter of anticipation, not reaction.

Exergy of a fire

A flame at 900 °C carries high-exergy heat; spending it to hold a 170 °C plate is a large irreversibility before any electricity is made. Combined heat and power is a rational answer: take the small electric share at the top of the temperature ladder and use the large remainder for space or water heating that would have been needed anyway.

That is the design logic of the commercial kits this board is built around: a controlled hot side, a water-cooled cold side, and the water going on to a heating loop.

For grown-ups

If you have a wood stove, an infrared thermometer on the top plate makes this chapter real. 150 to 170 °C is the band.

Chapter 4

Warm-upCould you get electricity out of a cup of coffee? Out of your hand?

Hot side, cold side

A thermoelectric module is a flat square. Make one face hot and the other cold and it makes electricity. Bigger difference, more electricity. No difference, nothing at all.

Put it on a stove, a camp fire, a cup of coffee, a sunny rock, or your own hand. Cool the other side with air, a fan, water, or ice. Find the biggest difference you can.

The Seebeck effect

Inside the module are dozens of tiny pairs of two different materials. When one end of a pair is hotter than the other, electrons drift toward the cold end and a voltage appears. Stack a hundred pairs in a row and you get a few volts from a difference of a hundred degrees. This is the Seebeck effect, found in 1821.

The difference across the module is never the difference between the source and the sink. The hot side has to get heat in and the cold side has to get heat out, and every joint costs some degrees. A good water-cooled cold side is worth more than a hotter fire.

V = S·ΔT and P ∝ ΔT²

Open-circuit voltage V = S ΔT, where S for a typical 40 mm module is about 0.04 V per degree. Into a matched load the power is P = V²/(4r) with r the internal resistance, about 2.5 Ω, so P ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ ΔT² W: 1.6 W at 100 °C of difference, 3.6 W at 150 °C. Power goes with the square of the difference.

The lab computes the module's share of the source-to-sink difference through a chain of thermal resistances, hot-side coupling, module, cold-side sink, so you can see why a fan or a water block changes the watts more than a hotter fire.

The thermal circuit of a module

ΔT_module = (T_source − T_sink) × R_m/(R_h + R_m + R_c), with R_m ≈ 1.5 K/W for a 40 mm Bi₂Te₃ module, R_h from 0.2 K/W (a clamped stove plate) to 10 K/W (a hand), and R_c from 0.05 K/W (ice bath) to 2 K/W (fins in still air). The heat through the module is that difference over R_m, and the electrical output is a few percent of it.

Everything scales together: a source that is hotter than the module's limit needs a spacer, which adds R_h; a sink that warms up loses its difference. The lab enforces the 170 °C hot-face limit that manufacturers quote for this class of module.

Coupled transport

The module obeys Q̇_h = S T_h I − ½I²r + K ΔT and Q̇_c = S T_c I + ½I²r + K ΔT: Peltier heat carried by the current, Joule heating split both ways, and Fourier conduction K ΔT that does no work. The Onsager relation ties the Peltier coefficient to the Seebeck coefficient, Π = S T, so the same material property governs both directions.

Interface resistances (electrical and thermal contact) and the temperature dependence of S, ρ and κ set real output below the textbook curve; the 0.04 V/K and 2.5 Ω used here are typical measured module values, not material constants.

For grown-ups

A single module on a candle can light an LED. The hand as a heat source gives about a tenth of a watt with a good sink, which is enough for a watch.

Chapter 5

Warm-upIf a fire makes 100 W of heat, why can't a machine turn all 100 W into electricity?

Why only five percent

A fire makes a great deal of heat, but a module turns only a tiny slice of it into electricity, about one part in twenty. The rest goes straight through as heat. That is not a mistake in the module. It is a rule about heat itself: you can only ever turn part of it into work, and the part depends on how big the difference is.

The good news: heat that goes through is still heat. Use it to warm water and nothing is wasted.

The Carnot limit

No machine can turn heat into work at better than a certain fraction, and the fraction depends only on the temperatures: 1 − T_cold/T_hot with both measured from absolute zero (add 273 to degrees Celsius). For a 170 °C hot side and a 0 °C cold side that limit is 1 − 273/443 = 38%. A thermoelectric module gets about a fifth of that, around 5 to 7%.

Why so far below the limit? Because the same material has to carry electricity well and heat badly, and almost nothing does both. That trade-off has a number, called ZT, and for the best everyday materials it is about 1.

Carnot times a ZT factor

Carnot: η_C = 1 − T_c/T_h. A thermoelectric generator at its best reaches η = η_C · (√(1+ZT) − 1)/(√(1+ZT) + T_c/T_h), where ZT = S²T/(ρκ) is the figure of merit. With ZT = 1, T_h = 443 K and T_c = 273 K: η_C = 0.38, the factor is 0.20, so η ≈ 7.8% ideal, and real modules land near 5%.

Move the sliders: a hotter hot side helps, a colder cold side helps more per degree, and ZT is the only lever that changes the factor.

The figure of merit

ZT = S²σT/κ bundles the three properties in tension: high Seebeck coefficient, high electrical conductivity, low thermal conductivity. Metals have high σ but tiny S; insulators the reverse; heavily doped semiconductors sit at the optimum. Bi₂Te₃ alloys reach ZT ≈ 1 near room temperature; skutterudites, half-Heuslers and PbTe-based alloys reach 1 to 1.5 at higher temperatures.

Combined heat and power changes the accounting: the exergy efficiency of electricity plus useful hot water can exceed 80% even though the electric share is 5%.

Seventy years at ZT ≈ 1

Ioffe's 1950s analysis set the framework; the wall has held because S, σ and κ_electronic are linked by the Wiedemann–Franz law and the Mott relation. Progress comes from cutting lattice thermal conductivity without hurting electrons: the phonon-glass electron-crystal idea (Slack), nanostructuring, hierarchical scattering, and band convergence, giving reported ZT of 2 to 2.5 in SnSe and PbTe systems at high temperature.

Module ZT lags material ZT because of contacts and the temperature spread across the legs; segmented legs and cascaded stages recover some of the difference at a cost in complexity.

For grown-ups

Kids accept the 5% readily; the surprising part is that it is a law, not a flaw. Carnot is the first equation most people meet that is a limit rather than a recipe.

Chapter 6

Warm-upWhy would plugging in the wrong thing make a generator produce less?

Match the load

The module makes electricity, but how much you get out depends on what you plug into it. Plug in something that lets too much through and the voltage collapses. Plug in something that lets too little through and hardly any flows. There is a sweet spot in the middle.

Slide the load until the watts peak. Then let the little box turn it into steady USB power.

Maximum power transfer

A module behaves like a battery with a resistor hidden inside, about 2.5 ohms. The most power comes out when the thing you connect has the same resistance as the hidden one. Bigger and the current starves; smaller and the voltage sags. Either way you lose.

Real devices do not want 3 volts that wobble. A boost converter takes whatever the module gives, holds the module at its sweet spot, and puts out a steady 5 V for USB, at the cost of a small slice of the power.

R_load = r

With open-circuit voltage V and internal resistance r, a load R draws current I = V/(R + r) and receives P = I²R = V²R/(R + r)². Differentiate and the maximum is at R = r, where P = V²/(4r) and the module's terminal voltage is exactly half of V. That is the number quoted in chapter 4.

A maximum-power-point tracker is a converter that adjusts its effective input resistance to stay at R = r as the temperature difference drifts. Modules in series add voltage; in parallel they add current; a kit rated 18 to 20 V under load is a series string.

Converters and tracking

A boost converter with efficiency η delivers P_out = η P_in at a fixed output voltage; its input impedance is set by duty cycle, which is the control variable for MPPT (perturb-and-observe or fractional open-circuit voltage, since V_mpp = V_oc/2 for a resistive source). Converter losses at low input voltage are the usual reason a 3 W module yields 2 W at USB.

Series strings are sensitive to one cold module: a module with a smaller ΔT limits the string's current, the same weak-cell problem as in battery packs.

Impedance matching as thermodynamics

Maximum power and maximum efficiency are different operating points: efficiency peaks at R/r = √(1+ZT) > 1, power at R/r = 1. A generator sized by fuel cost runs near maximum efficiency; one sized by module cost runs near maximum power. The lab lets you see both.

Electrical loading also changes the thermal circuit: drawing current moves Peltier heat across the module, lowering ΔT slightly, which is why the open-circuit ΔT overestimates the working one.

For grown-ups

The sweet spot exists for solar panels and batteries too. The idea transfers.

Chapter 7

Warm-upWhat would it take to charge a phone from a candle?

Build a heat-power system

Pick a fire, pick how many modules, pick how to keep the cold side cold, and see what you can run: a phone, a light, a radio. The leftover heat warms a tank of water, so nothing is thrown away.

Then try the candle and see why it cannot charge a phone. And watch the water: if it gets hot, the difference disappears.

From stove to phone

The sequence: what is the hot side temperature, what is the cold side, how many modules, what does each make, subtract the converter's slice, subtract the pump if there is one, and compare with the load. A phone charge is about 10 Wh; a 5 W USB output fills it in two hours. A power bank is 74 Wh.

The cold side is the whole game. Water carries heat away fast, but a small tank warms up; the difference shrinks and the watts fall. A bigger tank, or a loop that dumps the heat into a room, keeps it going. A commercial kit does exactly this and calls it combined heat and power.

Sizing the system

Per module P = 1.6 × 10⁻⁴ ΔT_module². Series string voltage is N × 0.02 × ΔT at the maximum power point. Converter efficiency ~85%, pump draw 1 to 3 W. Water tank heating: the module passes roughly 20 × its electrical output as heat, so 40 W electric means about 800 W into the water, warming 20 L by 34 °C per hour and collapsing the difference unless the heat is moved on.

The manufacturer rating this board uses as a worked example: 40 W peak with a 0 °C cold side and a 170 °C hot side, 18 to 20 V under load, active water cooling required. Reproduce that operating point in the lab and then see what a 20 °C cold side does to it.

Combined heat and power

Write the energy balance: fire → stove → module hot face (limit 170 °C) → module → cold block → water → tank or heating loop. Electric output is a few percent of the flow; the loop must reject the other 95% at a temperature useful for heating but low enough to keep the module's ΔT. A thermosiphon loop removes the pump's parasitic draw at the cost of flow control.

Warnings the lab raises are the real failure modes: hot face over limit, cold water over 60 °C, converter input below its start-up voltage, and a load larger than the output.

Where the numbers break

Peak ratings assume an ice-water cold side and a fully coupled hot side; a typical stove-top installation with fins and room air yields a third of nameplate. Long-term, thermal cycling fatigues solder joints and diffuses contacts; the tens-of-thousands-of-hours lifetime claims hold under steady thermal management, not repeated overshoot.

The honest design envelope: a well-built stove system delivers 10 to 40 W of electricity and hundreds of watts of hot water. That is enough for lights, radios, sensors and phones through a winter, and not enough for a refrigerator. The system checker on the Electricity board takes it from here.

For grown-ups

Try the candle first. Watching a phone need three days to charge is a better lesson than any sentence.

Chapter 8

Warm-upThe power just went out on a winter night. What do you turn on first, and what do you turn off?

Blackout

The power goes out. It is winter, it is night, and you have a stove, a pile of logs, a battery, and one long night to get through. Keep the lamp on. Charge the phone. Keep the room warm. Don't run out of wood.

Everything you learned is in this room. Cut the grid and see how you do.

Blackout: the whole system, live

When the grid is on, the wall charges the battery and the furnace warms the room. Cut it and the stove has to do both jobs. The plate must stay in its band for the modules to make watts, the water must stay cool for the difference to hold, and the battery must never hit empty or everything on the shelf goes dark.

Wood is limited. So is time. Decide what to run and when: the lamp all night, the phone before midnight, the radio for the morning news. Try the fridge and learn why nobody runs one on a stove.

Blackout: budgets and priorities

The numbers: eight modules make about 13 W usable at a 100 K difference, less the 2 W pump. The battery holds 200 Wh. The lamp is 5 W, 40 Wh for an eight-hour night; the phone needs 10 Wh; the radio 3 W; the fridge 60 W, which is 480 Wh a night and far beyond a stove. Each log is a kilogram, 16 MJ, about an hour of fire at a good burn.

The room is a second energy balance: the cabin loses about 100 W per degree above the outside air, and the stove gives it most of the fire. Play it twice: once to survive, once to finish with logs to spare.

Blackout as a scheduling problem

Two coupled balances, electrical and thermal, with one controllable input (fuel rate) and a set of switchable loads. The electrical side is a small storage system with a variable source; the thermal side has a fast state (the plate), a medium one (the tank) and a slow one (the room). The plate's 170 °C limit and the tank's 60 °C limit are constraints; wood is the budget.

Good play looks like good control: anticipate the plate's lag, stoke before it falls out of band, open the loop before the tank runs away, and shift loads to when the source is strong. Priority ordering under a fixed budget is the whole discipline of off-grid operation.

What a real winter setup needs

Storage sized for autonomy days rather than one night; a source that does not depend on someone awake to stoke it, which is why real installations pair a thermoelectric CHP unit with solar and a larger bank; telemetry loads that are small but never off; and protection against the failure modes this game exposes, plate overtemperature and cold-side runaway, as automatic interlocks rather than player attention.

At high latitude in midwinter, solar delivers a fraction of its summer output while heating demand peaks; a stove that yields tens of watts of electricity from heat you were making anyway is the rare resource that is strongest exactly when it is needed.

For grown-ups

Let a child run the night alone before you say anything. The lesson arrives when the battery hits empty at 3 a.m.

The end, for now

Make the difference, keep the difference, and take a small slice on the way through. Everything else is plumbing.
