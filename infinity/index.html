Georg Cantor's dangerous idea

Bigger than Infinity

Is infinity the biggest number? In 1874 Georg Cantor found out that some infinities are bigger than others. It made people so angry they tried to stop him. Here is how he did it, with games you can play.

In 1874 Georg Cantor proved that there are different sizes of infinity. Some mathematicians called it a disease. Today it is the foundation of mathematics. This page walks through his argument step by step, with games that let you check each step yourself.

Cantor's proof that the real numbers cannot be listed is one of the shortest deep proofs in mathematics. This page builds it from counting by matching, through Hilbert's Hotel and the zigzag through the fractions, to the diagonal argument and the question that turned out to have no answer.

Cardinality by bijection, countability of ℚ, uncountability of ℝ by diagonalization, Cantor's theorem for power sets, the aleph hierarchy, and the independence of the continuum hypothesis from ZFC. Each step has an interactive check.

From Cantor's 1874 and 1891 arguments to Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's forcing: what is provable, what is independent, and why the continuum problem, first on Hilbert's list, remains open in the sense that matters.

Chapter 1

Warm-upCan you tell if two piles are the same size without counting either?

Counting by matching

How do you know two piles have the same amount without counting? Pair them up. One apple, one cup. If nothing is left over, the piles are the same size. You never needed numbers at all.

Cantor's big idea: use pairing for infinite piles too, where counting would never finish.

Counting by matching

A pairing where every item on each side has exactly one partner is called a one-to-one match. Two collections have the same size exactly when such a match exists.

This definition works for finite piles, and it is the only definition that still makes sense for infinite ones. Try the uneven piles: one item is left over, so no match exists, so the sizes differ.

Bijections

A bijection between sets A and B is a function that is one-to-one and onto. Cantor defined |A| = |B| to mean that a bijection exists. For finite sets this agrees with counting; for infinite sets it is the definition.

|A| ≤ |B| means an injection A → B exists. The Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem says that injections both ways give a bijection, so ≤ behaves like an order.

Cardinality as an equivalence relation

Cardinality is the equivalence relation on sets defined by bijections; ≤ is defined by injections; Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein shows ≤ is antisymmetric, and with the axiom of choice it is total, so any two sets are comparable. Without choice, comparability can fail.

Note what the definition does not say: same size is not same shape. ℕ and ℤ are in bijection but not order-isomorphic. Cardinality forgets everything except matching.

Cardinality without choice

Hartogs' theorem gives, for any set X, an ordinal that does not inject into X, choice-free; comparability of all cardinals is equivalent to AC (Hartogs 1915). In ZF alone there are Dedekind-finite infinite sets: infinite, but with no countable subset and no bijection with a proper subset, so 'infinite' splits into inequivalent notions.

Cardinal arithmetic begins here: κ ≤ λ iff an injection exists, and κ² = κ for all infinite κ is itself equivalent to AC (Tarski 1924).

For grown-ups

The pairing idea is the whole foundation. Everything that follows is 'pair it up, and see whether anything is left over.'

Chapter 2

Warm-upA hotel with infinitely many rooms is full. Can it take one more guest?

Hilbert's Hotel

David Hilbert, 1924

Imagine a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on forever, and every room is full. A new guest arrives. No problem: everyone moves up one room, and room 1 is free.

A bus with infinitely many people arrives. Still no problem: everyone moves to double their room number, and all the odd rooms are free. A full hotel with room for more. That is what infinity is like.

Hilbert's Hotel

David Hilbert, 1924

David Hilbert used this hotel to show something strange: an infinite set can be matched one-to-one with part of itself. The guests in all the rooms match perfectly with the guests in only the even rooms. For finite piles that is impossible.

In fact that strangeness became the definition: a set is infinite exactly when it can be paired with a part of itself.

Countable infinity

David Hilbert, 1924

The whole numbers ℕ, the even numbers, the integers ℤ, and even ℕ × ℕ all have the same cardinality, written ℵ₀ (aleph-null). A set that can be paired with ℕ is called countable.

Dedekind turned the hotel trick into a definition: a set is infinite if it is in bijection with a proper subset of itself. The bus is the map n ↦ 2n; the infinitely many buses need a pairing of ℕ × ℕ with ℕ.

ℵ₀ and its arithmetic

David Hilbert, 1924

Countable unions of countable sets are countable (using countable choice), which is the infinitely-many-buses case: send passenger m of bus n to room 2ⁿ3ᵐ, or use the pairing function (n, m) ↦ (n + m)(n + m + 1)/2 + m.

So ℵ₀ + ℵ₀ = ℵ₀ · ℵ₀ = ℵ₀. Infinite cardinal arithmetic is degenerate: with choice, κ + λ = κ · λ = max(κ, λ).

Where choice hides

David Hilbert, 1924

The infinitely-many-buses step silently uses countable choice: to enumerate a countable union you must choose an enumeration of each set. In ZF alone, ℝ can be a countable union of countable sets (the Feferman–Levy model).

Dedekind-infinite versus infinite, and the dependence of cardinal arithmetic on AC, are the fault lines. The hotel is a theorem about ω; the buses are a theorem about choice.

For grown-ups

The hotel is a picture of the fact that the even numbers are 'as many' as all the whole numbers. Kids often find this outrageous. That is the right reaction; Cantor's contemporaries had it too.

Chapter 3

Warm-upBetween 1 and 2 there are endless fractions. So are there more fractions than whole numbers?

More fractions than whole numbers?

Cantor, 1874

Between any two whole numbers there are endless fractions. So there must be more fractions than whole numbers, right?

Cantor said: let's try to pair them. Write every fraction in a giant grid and walk through it in a zigzag. Every fraction gets a number on the way. So there are exactly as many fractions as whole numbers. Surprise.

More fractions than whole numbers?

Cantor, 1874

The zigzag visits every fraction eventually, so it gives each one a label: 1st, 2nd, 3rd. That is a one-to-one match between fractions and counting numbers. The fractions are countable.

Being densely packed does not make a set bigger than the counting numbers. Density and size are different things.

ℚ is countable

Cantor, 1874

List positive fractions p/q in a grid indexed by (p, q), walk the diagonals p + q = 2, 3, 4, ..., skip repeats like 2/4, and interleave negatives. Every rational appears at a finite step.

So |ℚ| = ℵ₀, despite ℚ being dense in ℝ. The same trick shows the set of all finite strings over a finite alphabet is countable, so there are only countably many computer programs.

Countable sets everywhere

Cantor, 1874

The algebraic numbers are countable (Cantor 1874): each is a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients, there are countably many such polynomials, and each has finitely many roots.

That was Cantor's first route to transcendental numbers, before the diagonal argument: ℝ is uncountable, the algebraic numbers are countable, so transcendentals exist, and in fact almost all reals are transcendental, without exhibiting a single one.

The 1874 proof

Cantor, 1874

Cantor's 1874 paper proves uncountability of ℝ by nested intervals, not diagonalization: given any sequence (xₙ), build nested closed intervals avoiding each xₙ; their intersection is nonempty by completeness. The 1891 diagonal argument is the one that generalized, to power sets, Gödel's incompleteness, the halting problem, and Russell's paradox.

The countability of the algebraic numbers is the enumeration half of the first non-constructive existence proof for transcendentals, five years after Hermite's constructive proof for e.

For grown-ups

The zigzag is a real proof, not a trick. The point for children is that 'more crowded' and 'more of them' are different ideas.

Chapter 4

Warm-upIf someone claims to have a list of every number between 0 and 1, how would you test it?

The diagonal trick

Cantor, 1891

Now try the same trick with all the numbers between 0 and 1, written as endless strings of digits. Suppose you had a list of all of them.

Cantor makes a new number: look at the first digit of the first number and change it. Then the second digit of the second number, and change it. And so on down the diagonal. The new number is different from every number on the list. So the list was never complete. No list can ever be. These numbers are a bigger infinity.

The diagonal trick

Cantor, 1891

Here is why the new number cannot be on the list. Is it the 1st number? No, its first digit is different. The 2nd? No, its second digit is different. It differs from the nth number at the nth digit, for every n.

Any list you write leaves something out, so the numbers between 0 and 1 cannot be paired with 1, 2, 3, .... They form a bigger infinity than the counting numbers.

The diagonal argument

Cantor, 1891

Suppose f: ℕ → {0,1}^ℕ lists every infinite binary sequence, with f(n) = (aₙ₁, aₙ₂, ...). Define b by bₙ = 1 − aₙₙ. Then b differs from f(n) in position n, for every n, so b is not on the list. Contradiction.

Hence the infinite binary sequences are uncountable, and so is ℝ (via binary expansions, taking care with dual representations such as 0.0111... = 0.1000...). The cardinality of ℝ is written 𝔠, or 2^ℵ₀.

Cantor's theorem

Cantor, 1891

The argument generalizes: for any set X there is no surjection X → 𝒫(X). Given g: X → 𝒫(X), let D = {x : x ∉ g(x)}; if D = g(d) then d ∈ D ⟺ d ∉ D.

The same shape appears in Russell's paradox, in Gödel's incompleteness theorem (a sentence saying 'I am unprovable'), in Turing's halting problem, and in the Y combinator. Lawvere's fixed-point theorem (1969) states the common form: in a cartesian closed category, a point-surjective map A → Y^A forces every endomorphism of Y to have a fixed point.

Diagonalization as a fixed-point theorem

Cantor, 1891

Lawvere's theorem shows Cantor, Russell, Gödel, Tarski and Turing are one theorem in different categories; Yanofsky (2003) gives the uniform treatment. Constructively the argument is fine: it proves that no surjection exists, an intuitionistically valid negative statement.

What needs more is the classical corollary that ℝ has a definite aleph as its size: in models of ZF without choice ℝ can fail to be well-orderable, so 𝔠 need not be an aleph at all.

For grown-ups

This is the proof that made Cantor famous and hated. The key sentence for a child: the new number is different from every number on the list, so the list was never complete.

Chapter 5

Warm-upIs there a biggest infinity?

The ladder, and the question nobody could answer

Is there a biggest infinity? No. Cantor showed how to always make a bigger one: take all the different groups you can make from a set. That collection is always bigger than the set you started with. So the infinities climb a ladder that never ends.

Then he asked: is there an infinity in between the counting numbers and the numbers on the line? He couldn't answer it. He spent years on it. The answer, found seventy years later, was the strangest of all: the question has no answer. Both yes and no fit all the rules of mathematics.

The ladder, and the question nobody could answer

A set with 3 things has 8 subsets. With 4 things, 16. In general 2ⁿ. Cantor proved the same holds for infinite sets: the subsets of a set always make a bigger infinity. So the infinities go up like a ladder: ℵ₀, then bigger, then bigger.

Cantor's question, the continuum hypothesis: is the infinity of the number line the very next rung after ℵ₀? In 1940 Kurt Gödel showed you cannot prove it false. In 1963 Paul Cohen showed you cannot prove it true. The usual rules of mathematics simply do not decide it.

Cantor's ladder and the continuum hypothesis

Cantor's theorem: |X| < |𝒫(X)| for every set X, so ℵ₀ < 2^ℵ₀ < 2^(2^ℵ₀) < .... The continuum hypothesis (CH) says 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁, the first uncountable cardinal: there is no set strictly between ℕ and ℝ in size.

Gödel (1940) proved CH is consistent with the standard axioms, ZFC. Cohen (1963) proved its negation is consistent too. CH is independent of ZFC. Hilbert had put it first on his 1900 list of problems.

Independence

Gödel built the constructible universe L, in which every set is definable from earlier stages; L satisfies ZFC + GCH, so CH cannot be refuted. Cohen invented forcing: adjoin ℵ₂ generic reals to a model of ZFC without collapsing cardinals, giving a model where 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂.

Easton's theorem shows 2^κ for regular κ can be almost anything consistent with König's theorem (cf(2^κ) > κ). ZFC says remarkably little about the continuum.

After independence

Independence was not the end. Woodin's Ω-logic program argued for ¬CH (2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂) from forcing axioms and the structure of H(ω₂); his later Ultimate-L program points toward CH. Forcing axioms such as PFA and MM imply 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₂. Large cardinals settle projective statements but not CH (Levy–Solovay).

Whether CH has a determinate truth value is a live question in the philosophy of mathematics: the multiverse view (Hamkins) against the universe view (Gödel's realism, Woodin). Set-theoretic geology and the modal logic of forcing are the modern tools.

For grown-ups

Kids like the ending: the greatest mathematicians could not answer the question, and eventually proved that it cannot be answered from the usual rules. 'No answer' is itself a discovery.

The end, for now

Cantor spent his last years in and out of hospital, sure he was right and mostly alone. He was right. The next rung is still waiting for someone to say what it looks like.

Cantor's ladder has no top.
