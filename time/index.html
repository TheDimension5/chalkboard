Time: everything has a heartbeat

The Pulse

Put your hand on your chest. That beat is your heart. The sea has a beat too: it comes in and goes out. Trees breathe in by day and out by night. Deep inside you, nerves flicker, and deeper still atoms wobble. Everything has a heartbeat. It just depends how far you look.

Every rhythm in nature is a pulse with its own length: a heartbeat is under a second, a tide about 12 hours, the planet's breath a year, a wave of light a couple of femtoseconds. Here every pulse beats at the same calm speed on screen, so you can feel it; what changes is how much time one beat holds. Dive from one beat to the next and count what fits inside.

Periods from the Planck time to the galactic year, one at a time. Each floor is a real oscillation or cycle with its period, from the cesium transition that defines the second (9,192,631,770 Hz) to the 100,000-year ice-age cycle. Moving between floors shows the ratio of periods, which is the honest way to read a log scale: not as exponents, but as how many of these fit inside one of those.

A ladder of periodic processes across about 60 orders of magnitude of time, with the frequency f = 1/T shown on demand. Biological rhythms sit near 1 Hz, geophysical ones (tides, days, seasons) are set by orbital mechanics, molecular and optical ones by quantum energy spacings, and the Planck time by ħ, G and c together, where no rhythm is known.

Oscillation frequencies are set by energy splittings (ω = ΔE/ħ) at the small end and by orbital dynamics (ω² ~ GM/r³) at the large end; the tidal period is the lunar day halved, the ice ages track Milankovitch forcing, and the cesium hyperfine line is the SI second itself. Tap along with any of them: entrainment, matching your own rhythm to one outside you, is the most physical way to feel a period.

Chapter 1

WonderPut your hand on your chest. How many things do you think happen inside one heartbeat?

Inside one heartbeat

Start with your own heart. Press down and dive inside one single beat. In that one beat, a hummingbird flaps its wings 42 times.

Dive inside one wingbeat and your nerves are flickering. Keep going: the atom inside every clock, a molecule wobbling, a wave of light, an electron circling. At the very bottom there is no beat at all, only a flicker nobody understands. Poke it.

Faster and faster pulses

Each floor down is a pulse that fits many times inside the one above it: 42 wingbeats in a heartbeat, 20 nerve pulses in a wingbeat, 9 million atomic-clock ticks in a nerve pulse. The screen always beats at the same speed, so you feel each rhythm the same way, but each beat is a smaller and smaller slice of time.

The atomic clock is special: the second itself is defined as 9,192,631,770 ticks of a cesium atom. Below the electron's lap there is a fall of about 10²⁷ before the Planck time, where no rhythm is known.

Period and frequency

A period T and a frequency f are the same fact: f = 1/T. A heartbeat is about 1.2 Hz, a hummingbird's wings about 50 Hz, the cesium clock 9.19 GHz, a C–H bond about 90 THz, green light 560 THz.

To see a fast rhythm you need a faster one to measure it with. That is why chemistry needed femtosecond laser flashes and electrons needed attosecond ones (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2023).

Energy sets the beat

At the small end, frequency is energy: f = ΔE/h. Molecular vibrations sit at tenths of an electronvolt (tens of femtoseconds), visible light at about 2 eV (a couple of femtoseconds), atomic binding at about 10 eV (tens of attoseconds). The cesium clock uses a tiny splitting, 38 micro-electronvolts, which is why it ticks at gigahertz.

Below about 10⁻²⁵ s there is no known rhythm down to the Planck time.

No rhythm at the bottom

t_P = √(ħG/c⁵) ≈ 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s. Probing it would take so much energy that the probe would collapse into a black hole of the same size, so neither quantum mechanics nor general relativity alone can say what happens there.

The board shows it as a flicker with no steady pulse and lets you poke it, because that is the honest state of knowledge.

For grown-ups

Have kids put a hand on their chest first and tap A in time with their own heart. Then dive. The sentence that matters on every floor is the one that appears between floors: how many of these fit inside one of those.

Chapter 2

WonderWhat in nature breathes, besides you?

Slower heartbeats

Now go the other way. Your breath is a slow heartbeat. The sea breathes too: for about six hours it comes in, for about six hours it goes out. Trees breathe in carbon dioxide by day and out at night.

Keep going and the whole planet breathes once a year, cicadas come up every 17 years, the ice comes and goes every 100,000 years, and the Sun takes 230 million years to go once round the galaxy.

Everything breathes

Rising up, each pulse holds many of the one below: 11,000 breaths in a tide, 30 days in a moon, 12 moons in a year. Every spring the northern forests grow leaves and pull carbon dioxide out of the air; every autumn it comes back. You can see that yearly breath in the air measured at Mauna Loa since 1958.

The slowest pulses are set by the sky: the Moon's pull makes the tides, slow wobbles in Earth's orbit pace the ice ages, and the galaxy's pull swings the Sun around its middle.

Cycles you can measure

Tides repeat every 12 hours 25 minutes because the Earth turns under two tidal bulges while the Moon moves on in its orbit. The yearly carbon dioxide cycle is about 6 parts per million peak to trough, riding on a long rise caused by burning fuel.

Periodical cicadas emerge every 13 or 17 years, both prime numbers; one idea is that prime cycles make it hard for predators to sync up with them.

Orbits set the slow beats

The large-scale rhythms are orbital: the lunar month, the tropical year, the Milankovitch cycles of eccentricity (about 100,000 years), tilt (41,000) and precession (about 23,000), and the Sun's galactic orbit of about 230 million years.

Each can be read from a record: tide gauges, the Keeling curve, tree rings, ice cores and ocean sediments hold these pulses in their layers.

From pulse to record

Slow periodic forcing leaves spectral peaks in climate records: the 100,000-year ice-age cycle is stronger than the eccentricity forcing alone predicts, one of the open puzzles of paleoclimate.

Above the galactic year there is no pulse we can see: the universe itself, 13.8 billion years old, has had only about 60 galactic years.

For grown-ups

Go outside afterwards: watch a tide table, a tree through a day, the Moon through a month. Every slow floor on this board is something you can see with your own eyes if you wait.

The end, for now

From the flicker at the Planck time to the Sun's slow lap of the galaxy, your own heart sits in the middle, beating about three billion times in a life.

Everything has a heartbeat. It depends how far you look.
