Chalk Kits

Every experiment on the boards has a twin you can do with things already in the room. Coins for the crash lab, a balloon for rockets, a ruler for the launch, a candle for the module. Each one takes minutes, and each points back to the chapter and its missions.

Launch Lab

Physics · 5 activities

pairs with chapter 1: Pushes and pulls · 10 min

Coin push

You need: A coin, a smooth table, a rough placemat or towel.

  1. Flick the coin across the bare table and measure how far it slides.
  2. Do the same push across the towel.
  3. Try a heavier coin with the same flick.

What to notice: The push ends at your finger; friction does the stopping. Rougher surface, shorter slide. The heavier coin needs a harder flick to reach the same speed but stops in about the same distance.

pairs with chapter 2: Everything falls the same · 5 min

Paper and book drop

You need: A hardback book, a sheet of paper.

  1. Drop the book and the paper side by side. The paper floats.
  2. Put the paper on top of the book and drop them together. They land together.
  3. Crumple the paper into a tight ball and drop it beside the book.

What to notice: The book shields the paper from the air, so gravity alone acts on both and they fall the same. Crumpling shrinks the paper's area so the air matters less.

pairs with chapter 3: The launch · 15 min

Ruler ramp and marble

You need: A ruler with a groove or a length of track, books to prop it, a marble, masking tape, a cup as the target.

  1. Prop the ruler on one book. Release the marble from the top and mark where it lands with tape.
  2. Add a second book. Mark the new landing spot.
  3. Put the cup where you predict the marble will land from three books. Test it.

What to notice: Higher start, faster marble, longer flight, but not double: the speed goes with the square root of the height. A heavier marble lands in the same place.

pairs with chapter 4: The crash lab · 5 min

Coin crash

You need: Two identical coins, one larger coin.

  1. Slide one coin into a resting identical coin. The mover stops; the other takes off.
  2. Slide the small coin into the large one. It bounces back.
  3. Slide the large coin into the small one. Both keep going.

What to notice: Equal masses swap motion. A light thing bouncing off a heavy one, and a heavy thing barely noticing a light one, are momentum conservation with no equations.

pairs with chapter 6: Rockets · 10 min

Balloon rocket

You need: A balloon, a drinking straw, string, tape.

  1. Thread the string through the straw and tie it across the room, slightly uphill.
  2. Blow up the balloon, hold it closed, tape it to the straw.
  3. Let go.

What to notice: The balloon pushes air backward; the air pushes the balloon forward. It is not pushing on the room air: it would work in a vacuum. Ask what runs out first, air or string.

Hot Side, Cold Side

Engineering · 4 activities

pairs with chapter 1: Heat flows downhill · 5 min

Spoon in hot water

You need: A metal spoon, a wooden spoon, a plastic spoon, a mug of hot tap water.

  1. Stand all three spoons in the mug.
  2. After a minute, touch each handle.

What to notice: Metal carries heat fast, wood and plastic barely at all. That is the bar between the two blocks. Which spoon would you make a heat sink from?

pairs with chapter 2: Boiling water · 10 min

Time the kettle

You need: A kettle or pot, a measuring jug, a stopwatch, the kettle's wattage from its label.

  1. Boil exactly one litre from cold and time it.
  2. Repeat with half a litre.
  3. Compare with 4,186 J per kg per degree divided by the label wattage.

What to notice: Half the water takes roughly half the time. The measured time is a little longer than the arithmetic because the kettle warms up and loses heat as it goes.

pairs with chapter 4: Hot side, cold side · 10 min

Hand-warmed module

You need: A thermoelectric module (a few dollars online), an LED or a small motor, a bowl of ice water.

  1. Press the module between your palm and a cold metal surface or a bowl of ice water.
  2. Connect the LED. Watch for a glow. Swap the wires if not.
  3. Try a cup of hot water on the top instead of your hand.

What to notice: Your hand is the hot side at 33 °C. The bigger the difference, the brighter the glow. A module on a candle can light an LED easily; the same module on a warm hand needs a very cold other side.

pairs with chapter 5: Why only five percent · 5 min

Where the heat goes

You need: A running laptop or phone, your hand.

  1. Feel the back of a laptop that has been working hard.
  2. Ask: the laptop used 30 W of electricity; where is it now?

What to notice: All of it became heat. Turning electricity into heat is easy and complete; turning heat back into electricity is hard and partial. That asymmetry is the second law.

Electricity From Zero

Engineering · 4 activities

pairs with chapter 1: Watts and watt-hours · 10 min

Read the labels

You need: A phone charger, a light bulb box, a power bank, the electricity bill.

  1. Find the watts on the charger and the bulb.
  2. Find the watt-hours (or mAh and volts) on the power bank.
  3. Find the kWh on the bill.

What to notice: Every number is either a rate (W) or an amount (Wh). Sort them into two columns. Then work out how many hours the power bank could run the bulb.

pairs with chapter 2: Push, flow, opposition · 10 min

Battery, bulb, wire

You need: A AA battery, a flashlight bulb, a wire, a pencil lead.

  1. Light the bulb with one wire, the battery and the bulb touching.
  2. Put the pencil lead in the circuit and slide the wire along it.

What to notice: The lead is a resistor. More lead in the path, dimmer bulb: more opposition, less flow. This is the ohm slider in your hand.

pairs with chapter 5: Series and parallel · 5 min

Stack the batteries

You need: Two AA batteries, a 3 V LED, tape.

  1. Try to light the LED with one battery. Nothing, or very dim.
  2. Tape two batteries end to end and try again.
  3. Put the batteries side by side (same ends together) and try again.

What to notice: End to end the pushes add and the LED lights. Side by side the push is the same as one battery, so it stays dim, but they would last twice as long.

pairs with chapter 9: What actually moves · 5 min

The full hose

You need: A garden hose already full of water, or a straw full of water with a finger over the end.

  1. Push water in one end.
  2. Watch water come out the other end immediately.

What to notice: The water that came out was not the water you pushed. Electrons in a wire work the same way: the wire is full, and the push travels far faster than the stuff.

One Rule for Everything

Physics · 3 activities

pairs with chapter 2: Gravity is a dent · 15 min

Bedsheet gravity

You need: A bedsheet or stretchy fabric, four people to hold it, a heavy ball, marbles.

  1. Hold the sheet taut and flat. Roll a marble across: straight line.
  2. Put the heavy ball in the middle. Roll a marble past it.
  3. Roll a marble sideways fast enough to circle the ball.

What to notice: The marble is not pulled by the ball; it follows the dent. Faster sideways roll makes a longer orbit. Everything the simulation does, the sheet does, except time.

pairs with chapter 3: Magnets have invisible hair · 10 min

Iron filings and a magnet

You need: A bar magnet, iron filings or steel wool cut fine, a sheet of paper.

  1. Put the paper over the magnet and sprinkle the filings.
  2. Tap the paper.
  3. Move a compass around the edge.

What to notice: The filings draw the field lines the simulation computes. The compass points along them. Nothing about the paper bent; the pattern sits on top of it.

pairs with chapter 9: The puzzle is still open · 10 min

Two rulebooks on one desk

You need: A magnet, a paper clip, a small ball.

  1. Drop the ball: gravity. Lift the clip with the magnet: magnetism.
  2. Try to pick the ball up with the magnet. Try to drop the clip past the magnet.

What to notice: Two pulls on one desk that ignore each other completely. That is what Einstein could not stand.

Bigger than Infinity

Mathematics · 3 activities

pairs with chapter 1: Counting by matching · 5 min

Cups and spoons

You need: A pile of cups, a pile of spoons.

  1. Without counting, put one spoon in each cup.
  2. Whatever is left over tells you which pile was bigger.

What to notice: Matching decides same-size without numbers. This is the whole tool Cantor used for infinite piles.

pairs with chapter 2: Hilbert's Hotel · 10 min

The hallway hotel

You need: A hallway of chairs, a class of students.

  1. Every chair has a student. A new student arrives.
  2. Everyone moves one chair down. Where does the last student go?
  3. Now say the hallway never ends.

What to notice: With a real hallway someone falls off the end. With an endless one, nobody does, and there is always a free chair. Infinite means the trick never fails.

pairs with chapter 4: The diagonal trick · 10 min

Beat the list

You need: Paper, a partner.

  1. Your partner writes five rows of five random 0s and 1s and claims it is every possible row.
  2. You write a new row by flipping the first digit of row one, second of row two, and so on.
  3. Show it is not on their list. Let them add it. Do it again.

What to notice: The list can never catch up. That is the diagonal argument played as a game.

How History Survives

History · 4 activities

pairs with chapter 1: The copying game · 15 min

Scribe chain

You need: A paragraph on paper, six students, pens.

  1. Student one copies the paragraph by hand and passes only their copy on.
  2. Each student copies the copy they received.
  3. Compare the last copy with the original.

What to notice: Errors accumulate and are inherited. Find one mistake that every later copy shares: it tells you where in the chain it was born.

pairs with chapter 2: Family trees of books · 15 min

Find the family

You need: Five copies of a sentence prepared by the teacher with planted shared errors.

  1. Give each group all five copies.
  2. Ask them to draw the family tree from shared mistakes and reconstruct the original.

What to notice: Groups discover on their own that three copies can be wrong together if they descend from one bad parent. Count branches, not copies.

pairs with chapter 4: How old is it? · 10 min

Halving pennies

You need: 64 pennies, a table.

  1. Each round, remove half the pennies. Count rounds until one is left.
  2. Ask how many rounds ago there were 64.

What to notice: Six halvings from 64 to 1. Carbon-14 does this every 5,730 years, and the pennies left tell you the rounds elapsed. That is the whole dating method.

pairs with chapter 5: Bits rot too · 10 min

Three copies vote

You need: Three copies of a short text, one with a planted error in each copy at different places.

  1. Compare the three line by line.
  2. Wherever they disagree, take the majority.

What to notice: You recovered the original with no copy being perfect. This is exactly what the copies-and-voting switch does to the picture.
